Here is why taking and analyzing those retinal images is not a waste of time:

Good control is not a 100% guarantee: You are right that taking treatments and maintaining a strict diet massively lowers the risk. However, the longer a person has diabetes (say, 10 or 20 years), the higher their risk becomes, even if they have controlled it perfectly.

The "Silent" Damage: This is the most critical point. In its early and treatable stages, diabetic retinopathy has zero symptoms. A patient's vision will seem perfectly normal to them, and they will feel fine, even while tiny blood vessels are actively leaking inside their eye.

The Point of No Return: If doctors wait to act until the patient finally says, "My vision is getting blurry," the damage is often severe, permanent, and much harder to treat.

The Only Way to Know: Because there are no early warning signs, the only way to catch the disease in time to save the person's sight is to take a picture of the retina and literally look for those microscopic leaks.

Why the AI is needed:
Every single person with diabetes is supposed to get this eye photo taken once a year. There are hundreds of millions of people with diabetes worldwide, but there are not nearly enough eye doctors to manually look at all those millions of photos. 

To answer your first question: the specific picture required is called a retinal fundus photograph. It is a magnified, highly detailed picture of the inside back of the eye (the retina), which shows the blood vessels, the optic nerve, and the macula.

To answer your second question: Yes, it is absolutely possible to get these images in rural and remote areas today, and that is exactly where this AI project shines.

Here is why it works in remote areas:

The Old Way (Not rural-friendly): In the past, taking a fundus photograph required a massive, expensive, heavy machine that sat on a desk. You could only find these in large city hospitals or specialized eye clinics, meaning rural patients had to travel far just to get screened.

The New Way (Rural-friendly): Today, technology has advanced massively. We now have two highly portable ways to capture these images:

Handheld Fundus Cameras: These are small, battery-operated devices that a health worker can easily pack into a bag and take to a remote village.

Smartphone-Based Imaging: This is a game-changer. Engineers have created small, low-cost lenses and adapters that attach directly to a standard smartphone. A local nurse or community health worker can point their smartphone at the patient's eye, use the camera flash, and capture a high-quality picture of the retina.

How your AI fits perfectly into this:In a remote village, a local health worker can use a smartphone adapter to snap a picture of a diabetic patient's eye. Even if there is no eye doctor within 100 miles, the health worker can feed that image into your AI system.
